From flax field to folded sheet.

Six steps, four of them under our own roof. We own the looms, the dye house, the wash floor and the sewing room, which is why we can tell you what happened to your sheets at every one of them.

01 of 06Normandy, France

0

0 litres of irrigation

Flax

Our yarn is spun from flax grown on the Channel coast, where rain and sea air do the work irrigation does elsewhere. Flax needs no watering and almost no pesticide. We buy long-line fibre only, which is the part that makes smooth, strong yarn.

02 of 06Jiangsu, China

24

24 Nm yarn count

Spinning

The fibre is wet-spun: drawn through warm water so the natural gums soften and the yarn comes out fine and even. Dry-spun yarn is cheaper and hairier. You can feel the difference with your eyes shut.

03 of 06Keqiao, Shaoxing

46

46 looms

Weaving

Forty-six rapier looms run in two shifts in our own shed. Sheeting is a plain weave at 165 grams per square metre: heavy enough to last, light enough to sleep under in August.

04 of 06Keqiao, Shaoxing

300

300 metres per dye lot

Dyeing

Cloth is dyed in the piece, in lots of 300 metres, with reactive dyes that bond to the fibre. Each lot is checked against the master shade under daylight lamps. Indigo is the one colour we still let bleed a little; it is meant to fade like denim.

05 of 06Keqiao, Shaoxing

2

2 washes before cutting

Washing

This is where the softness comes from. Every metre is washed twice in softened water with enzymes, then tumbled dry. It shrinks here, on our floor, so it does not shrink on your bed.

06 of 06Keqiao, Shaoxing

62

62 cutters and sewers

Cutting and sewing

Sixty-two people cut and sew in the building next to the looms. Seams are double-stitched, corners are mitred, and every piece is checked on a light table before it is folded.

Then it goes in a box, to you.
